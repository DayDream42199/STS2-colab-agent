Slay the Spire 2 co-op agent · results up to 7 November 2026

Does adapting to teammates pay?

A little, so far. We now have a much stronger agent, and it clearly changes who it protects according to each teammate's style. That change barely moves wins or score: with the right profiles, both teammates survive about 2 points more often, and a recheck on new fights narrowly missed our bar.

Decision needed: our aim is an agent players are comfortable playing with. We now have a proposed definition of comfort and first measures from simulated fights, but only real players can settle it. We need to agree the definition and plan real games. The options are at the end of this page.

The agent is much stronger than it was

Two changes did most of this. A new network gives every card-and-target pair one shared scorer, replacing a network that learned each seat separately and formed seat habits. A reward that charges more for teammates' HP loss and knockouts then added a little more, mostly on one seed. The best agent so far uses both, after 1M training steps.

Win rate and both teammates alive, next to two teammates of one style

1,000 fights per teammate style and HP setting. Differences between two agents are good to about ±2.5 to ±3 points at low HP. Every loss is a wipe, so winning more also keeps more teammates alive: about three quarters of the pair network's first survival gain came from winning more. The best agent is the top of 15 tested on the same fights, so its numbers are probably slightly flattering.

It now reacts to the profile, but results barely move

Lift and Intercept protect one chosen teammate. We gave the agent one aggressive and one defensive teammate, told it their styles correctly or the wrong way round, and counted who got these cards. Every pair-network agent sends most of them to whoever it is told is aggressive, so swapping the labels moves the cards to the other player. The old network ignores the label.

Rules on who to protect add no clear gain, and a rule on when removes most of the waste

We kept the agents' timing and forced only the target of Lift and Intercept, to the lower-HP or the higher-HP teammate. Card counts per fight stayed the same, so this shows the effect of the target alone. Sending everything to the wrong teammate clearly hurts (both alive +5.2 ± 1.5 for lower against higher). Sending everything to the lower-HP teammate adds no clear gain over the agent's own choice (+0.8 ± 1.5 on same-style teams, +0.4 ± 1.2 on mixed teams).

Rules on who gets the cards change little because in most wasted plays neither teammate needs protecting. The large fall in covered plays is built into the timing rule, so what matters is its cost. With the rule the agent spends the freed turns mostly on Wait, Strike, ending its turn and Defend. A wider check on 12,000 more fights showed no clear change on same-style teams at low HP (both alive −0.4 ± 2.1, win +0.1 ± 1.7) or across all teams at full HP (both alive +0.0 ± 0.5, win +0.1 ± 0.2), so the win gain above did not repeat. That check narrowly missed its bar, which needed no clear fall on either agent in either group: at full HP the best agent lost 7 of 4,000 fights instead of 1. The rule may therefore have a very small cost.

What comfort could mean

A collaborative agent can score below a general agent and still be the better teammate. We propose this definition for the team to agree or change: a player can play their own way without having to worry about the bot. It splits into five feelings. Simulated fights can show signs of some of them, and whether those signs track comfort is untested.

Most margins are about ±1 point. The covered row doesn't count a teammate protected by someone's Intercept; counting that gives 35–45% for the two 1M agents on mixed teams at low HP. In a later check of those plays, the other teammate was safe too in 97% of covered Lifts and 89% of covered Intercepts, and no attack was coming at all in 62% and 46%, so the waste is mainly about when the agent plays these cards. Unused Block is Block still on a player when the enemy turn ends, charged to whoever added Block to that player last in the round. It isn't always a mistake, since a card left in hand is discarded anyway. A Wait lets each teammate take one action, and with a real player it would mean a pause of unknown length. The scripted teammates block without checking what is coming, so real players will differ.

How we test it

Agent
A MaskablePPO network playing one seat on our port of the Act 1 engine. It trains next to one to three scripted teammates, and the tests here mostly use two.
Best agent
The pair network with the survival reward after 1M training steps, seed 1. The reward's collaboration bonus is off.
Profile
Three numbers built from a teammate's plays: Aggression (attack or Block), Scaling (invest or play for now) and Teamwork (help others or self).
Advantage
The agent's result when shown each teammate's true profile, minus its result when shown the other style's profile.
Score
100 minus penalties for losing, HP lost, knockouts, enemy HP left and long fights, always on the default weights so that runs with different rewards compare. Scores only compare at the same starting HP.
Survival reward
Charges a teammate's HP loss and knockouts more than the agent's own during training.
Low HP
Each player starts at 100, 100, 75, 50 or 25% of max HP, drawn per player per fight. This stands in for the game carrying HP between fights.
Rules
Wait lets each teammate take one action before the agent moves. The scripted teammates never wait back.
Fights
Act 1 elite and boss fights with a fixed 24-card deck in every seat. Teammates are bots that favour attacks or favour Block.

Earlier results, from before the card fix

These used the older network and the misdescribed cards, so they are not final. Their main points still guide the work.

  • Knowing a style added no points. Advantage was −0.10 ± 0.28 under normal rules and −0.08 ± 0.59 without Wait.
  • Switching to the right specialist added almost nothing, at most about +0.3 points across all 10 test teams with or without Wait.
  • Training at low HP did not help. Those agents scored level or lower and still didn't favour the weaker teammate.
  • Seeing teammates act first is worth a lot. Removing Wait cost the agents 5.8 points and the Cheese bot 1.1. On a carried-HP route the general agent cleared 72% with Wait and 32% without. Real players may not let the agent move after them. Part of this gain may be the extra energy described under the limits below, because Wait lets the agent use it the same turn.
  • Carried HP makes fights far harder. Boss win rate fell from 82–89% to 41–56% next to aggressive teammates, and from 54–76% to 33–53% next to defensive ones.
  • One result is still open. With hidden hands on boss fights, each specialist beat the other next to its own teammate type, on one seed only.

Who older agents protected

Lift and Intercept are the cards that protect one chosen teammate. When both teammates are alive at different HP, trained agents play them on the lower one about half the time, close to the Cheese bot. A simple scripted bodyguard does so 71 to 84% of the time, so there is a real choice to make.

What limits these results

  • All teammates are scripted bots. We have no real-player data. The bots don't react to the agent or to their own HP, and they never wait back unless a test makes them.
  • The scripted teammates favour the agent. When a bot's choices tie, it takes the first seat, which is the agent. So they aim almost all their Believe in You at it (about 20% extra energy) and 85–93% of their Lift and Intercept. Breaking those ties at random costs the two 1M agents 1.7 and 2.5 points of low-HP win and almost nothing at full HP, so the results above hold up but are slightly flattered. The agents were still trained next to the old bots, and we haven't re-measured damage shares or the old network with fairer bots.
  • Two training seeds per agent, and the margins count fight luck only. Seeds still differ: one 1M seed holds back 39% of its cards until teammates finish, and the other 14%. An agent's strength also swings between training updates, and we haven't measured how much.
  • True against swapped compares right information with wrong information. No pair agent has been trained without profiles, so we can't say how much the profile helps compared with having none.
  • One fixed deck, elite and boss fights only, one fight at a time. Low starting HP stands in for HP carried through a run.
  • The collaboration bonus is off on every result above. It has only been tried on older random decks and the old network.
  • One card is still blank in the agent's input. Believe in You has no number for the energy it gives.

Decision for the team

The planned week-long sweep stays on hold, because it was built to tune score. The options are in the order we suggest. The first two come first because comfort is our goal and everything so far is simulated.

  1. Agree what success means.

    Accept or change the five feelings above, decide how we will measure them, and decide how much score we will give up for them. For example, the agent could be required to stay above the scripted Cheese bot, which it now clears easily.

  2. Plan real games.

    Playing with real people is the only direct test of comfort, and the logs would let us calibrate the bots. A terminal playtest is ready now: a person plays next to the agent without knowing which version it is, rates each fight, and can run it with python playtest.py --name Sam. It can't show how waiting feels in real time. Full games need the agent connected to the live game, which isn't built yet, and a decision on how long it should wait for a real player. All of this depends on people's time.

  3. Decide whether the live agent uses the timing rule.

    It removes about 90 to 95% of the wasted Lift and Intercept plays on every team and HP level tested. The one measured cost is very small: at full HP the best agent lost 7 of 4,000 fights instead of 1. Real games would show whether players notice the difference.

  4. Train a pair agent without profiles.

    This shows how much the profile helps compared with having none, which true against swapped can't show. About two hours of training for two seeds, with the bots' tie-break fixed first, as any new training should have.

  5. Try the collaboration bonus on the pair network.

    Judge it on the agreed measure, with its weights measured on this deck first. A few hours of training.

  6. Settle the open hidden-hands result.

    It came before the card fix, so it now needs both specialists retrained. This matters less if score stops being the main measure.
